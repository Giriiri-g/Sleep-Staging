\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $n \geq 0 \vee x \neq 0$
\Ensure $y = x^n$ 
\State $y \Leftarrow 1$
\If{$n < 0$}\label{algln2}
        \State $X \Leftarrow 1 / x$
        \State $N \Leftarrow -n$
\Else
        \State $X \Leftarrow x$
        \State $N \Leftarrow n$
\EndIf
\While{$N \neq 0$}

            \State $N \Leftarrow N / 2$
        \Else[$N$ is odd]
            \State $y \Leftarrow y \times X$
            \State $N \Leftarrow N - 1$
        \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}

\section{Cross referencing}\label{sec8}

Environments such as figure, table, equation and align can have a label
declared via the \verb+\label{#label}+ command. For figures and table
environments use the \verb+\label{}+ command inside or just
below the \verb+\caption{}+ command. You can then use the
\verb+\ref{#label}+ command to cross-reference them. As an example, consider
the label declared for Figure~\ref{fig1} which is
\verb+\label{fig1}+. To cross-reference it, use the command 
\verb+Figure \ref{fig1}+, for which it comes up as
``Figure~\ref{fig1}''. 

To reference line numbers in an algorithm, consider the label declared for the line number 2 of Algorithm~\ref{algo1} is \verb+\label{algln2}+. To cross-reference it, use the command \verb+\ref{algln2}+ for which it comes up as line~\ref{algln2} of Algorithm~\ref{algo1}.

\section{Methods}\label{sec11}

This study adopts a structured end-to-end methodological pipeline for detecting sleep disorders from overnight polysomnography (PSG) recordings. The proposed framework was iteratively developed and refined through multiple experimental trials exploring different strategies for signal preprocessing, feature extraction, sequence modelling, explainability, and classification. The overall pipeline is organized into five primary modules. First, the data processing module performs channel selection, signal preprocessing, and data representation tailored for PSG time-series analysis. Second, the temporal feature extraction module learns discriminative representations from physiological signals while addressing limitations observed in prior work. Third, the sequence modelling module employs a lightweight attention-based transformer with contextual memory to capture temporal dependencies between sleep epochs and perform sleep staging. Fourth, the disorder classification module analyzes features aggregated across the full-night recording to identify the presence of sleep disorders. Finally, an explainable inference module provides interpretability by localizing important signal regions within epochs using attention-based importance mapping, estimating prediction uncertainty through confidence visualization, and utilizing entropy-based signaling to flag low-confidence epochs for potential manual review by clinicians. The complete pipeline is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:methodology}.

\subsection{Dataset Description}
This study utilizes two large-scale, publicly available, polysomnography (PSG) datasets: the Sleep-EDF Expanded Dataset\cite{sleepedfx, physiobank} and the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) Sleep Dataset\cite{mesa, nsrr}. These datasets were selected to support the two-stage objective of this work: (i)  sleep stage classification from PSG signals, and (ii) association of sleep architecture with common sleep disorders. The Sleep-EDF Expanded dataset is used for training and validation of the sleep staging model, while the MESA dataset is employed for disorder-level analysis and for evaluating cross-dataset generalization.

\subsubsection{Sleep-EDF Expanded Dataset}

The Sleep-EDF Expanded dataset\cite{sleepedfx} was obtained from PhysioNet\cite{physiobank}, originally published in 2018 as version 1.0.0. The dataset consists of whole-night PSG recordings collected as part of two longitudinal studies: Sleep-Cassette (SC) and Sleep-Telemetry (ST). The SC subset includes recordings from healthy subjects without sleep-related medication, while the ST subset contains recordings from subjects with difficulty initiating sleep, with nights recorded under both placebo and temazepam conditions.

Across both subsets, the dataset comprises recordings from over 100 Caucasian adult subjects, spanning an age range of approximately 25 to 101 years, with each subject contributing one or more overnight recordings. In total, the expanded dataset contains  197 recorded nights, with each night lasting approximately 8 hours.

PSG data were acquired using standard laboratory-grade equipment, including EEG (Fpz–Cz and Pz–Oz electrode derivations), electrooculogram (EOG), and chin electromyogram (EMG) signals. Both EEG and EOG channels were sampled at 100 Hz. In this work, only the Fpz–Cz EEG channel was used to maintain consistency across datasets and to enable single-channel sleep staging.

Sleep stages were manually annotated by trained sleep experts following the Rechtschaffen and Kales (R\&K) standard. Each recording was segmented into 30-second epochs, with labels assigned to one of the following stages: Wake (W), N1, N2, N3, N4, REM, Movement Time, and Unknown. In accordance with current AASM conventions, N4 epochs were merged into N3, and non-physiological stages (Movement Time and Unknown) were excluded during preprocessing.

\subsubsection{MESA Sleep Dataset}

The MESA Sleep dataset\cite{mesa} was accessed through the National Sleep Research Resource (NSRR)\cite{nsrr} and was released in 2016 as part of the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis. Unlike Sleep-EDF, MESA is a large-scale, population-based cohort designed to investigate the relationship between sleep characteristics and cardiometabolic health outcomes.

The dataset includes over 2,200 middle-aged and older adults (aged approximately 45–84 years) from diverse ethnic backgrounds, with each participant undergoing one full-night in-home PSG recording. PSG acquisition followed standardized protocols and included multi-channel EEG, EOG, EMG, respiratory airflow, oxygen saturation, and electrocardiogram (ECG) signals, sampled at frequencies ranging from 100 to 256 Hz depending on the modality.

Sleep stages in MESA were manually scored in 30-second epochs by certified sleep technologists according to AASM guidelines. In addition to sleep staging, MESA provides clinically adjudicated labels for several common sleep disorders, making it suitable for disorder-level analysis.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}\label{subsec:preprocessing}

Two distinct preprocessing pipelines were implemented to handle the different characteristics of the Sleep-EDF and MESA datasets. The Sleep-EDF pipeline produces epoch-level tensors for sleep staging model training, while the MESA pipeline computes subject-level clinical feature vectors for disorder classification.

\subsubsection{Sleep-EDF Signal Processing}

Raw PSG recordings in EDF format were processed using the MNE-Python library. From the available polysomnography channels, only the EEG Fpz--Cz derivation was retained for analysis. This single-channel approach was selected to ensure consistency across datasets and to reduce computational complexity while preserving the frequency content necessary for sleep stage discrimination.

Sleep stage annotations were loaded from separate hypnogram EDF files containing expert-scored labels. Stage mapping followed current AASM conventions: Wake (W), N1, N2, N3, and REM. Stage N4 was merged into N3, and non-physiological labels (Movement Time, Unknown) were excluded from training.

Continuous EEG signals were segmented into non-overlapping 30-second epochs, yielding 3,000 samples per epoch at the 100 Hz sampling rate. The 30-second epoch duration follows the standard Rechtschaffen and Kales scoring convention and enables direct comparison with clinical sleep staging practice.

Recordings were temporally trimmed to focus on sleep-containing segments. Wake epochs at recording boundaries were removed, retaining a 30-minute buffer before sleep onset and after final awakening. This reduces class imbalance by excluding extended waking periods unrelated to sleep architecture analysis.

Processed recordings were saved as PyTorch tensors with shape $[n_{\text{epochs}} \times 3000]$, where each row corresponds to one 30-second epoch. An index file records the tensor path and stage sequence for each recording.

\subsubsection{Spectral Feature Extraction}

For each 30-second epoch, complementary spectral features were computed from the EEG signal to provide frequency-domain information alongside raw waveform input.

Discrete wavelet transform features were extracted using the Daubechies-4 (db4) wavelet at decomposition level 5. For each coefficient level, four statistics were computed: energy, log-energy, Shannon entropy, and variance. The db4 wavelet was selected for its morphological similarity to sleep spindle and K-complex waveforms.

Short-time Fourier transform features were computed using a Hann window with 2-second window length and 50 percent overlap. Relative power was calculated for canonical frequency bands: Delta (0.5--4 Hz), Theta (4--8 Hz), Alpha (8--13 Hz), Beta (13--30 Hz), and Gamma (30--45 Hz). Additional spectral metrics include spectral entropy computed as $H = -\sum p_k \log p_k$ where $p_k$ is the normalized power at frequency bin $k$, and spectral edge frequency at 95 percent (SEF95) defined as the frequency below which 95 percent of total power resides.

Ratio features capture clinically relevant spectral relationships: delta-to-beta ratio, theta-to-alpha ratio, and combined slow-to-fast power ratio $((\text{delta} + \text{theta}) / (\text{alpha} + \text{beta}))$.

The complete spectral feature vector contains approximately 34 features per epoch, saved as separate tensor files aligned with raw signal epochs.

\subsubsection{MESA Clinical Feature Computation}

For the MESA dataset, a comprehensive clinical feature extraction pipeline was implemented to compute over 130 features across seven physiological domains from full PSG recordings.

EDF files were loaded via MNE with automatic channel identification using alias matching. High-frequency channels (EEG, ECG, EMG, EOG) with original sampling rates of 256 Hz were downsampled to 100 Hz using polyphase anti-alias filtering. Sleep stage annotations were parsed from NSRR-format XML files containing scored events with start time and duration fields.

Sleep architecture features include total sleep time, time in bed, sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, and sleep efficiency computed as $(\text{TST} / \text{TIB}) \times 100$. Stage-specific metrics include absolute duration and proportion for each stage, N3-to-N2 ratio, light-to-deep sleep ratio, REM latency, total stage transitions, transition rate per hour, and transition entropy.

Respiratory features follow AASM event detection criteria. Airflow signals were bandpass filtered (0.05--4 Hz) and the RMS envelope computed with 3-second RMS windows. Apneas were detected as 90 percent or greater amplitude reduction lasting 10 seconds or longer. Hypopneas were detected as 30 percent or greater reduction with associated desaturation. Events were classified as obstructive versus central based on respiratory effort channel amplitude during the event. Derived metrics include AHI, AI, HI, obstructive apnea index, central apnea index, REM-specific AHI, and mean and maximum apnea duration.

Oxygenation features from SpO2 analysis include mean saturation, nadir, standard deviation, T90 (time below 90 percent), T88 (time below 88 percent), ODI3 and ODI4 desaturation indices, and desaturation area-under-curve computed as the integral of deficit below 90 percent.

Cardiac and heart rate variability features were computed from ECG using R-peak detection via a Pan--Tompkins-inspired algorithm. Time-domain metrics include SDNN (standard deviation of NN intervals), RMSSD (root mean square of successive differences), and mean RR interval. Frequency-domain metrics include LF power (0.04--0.15 Hz), HF power (0.15--0.40 Hz), and LF/HF ratio via cubic spline interpolation of RR series to 4 Hz.

Periodic limb movement features were derived from leg EMG following AASM criteria: burst amplitude threshold of 8 times baseline, duration 0.5--10 seconds, inter-movement interval 5--90 seconds, and PLM series defined as 4 or more consecutive movements. Features include PLM count, PLMI (index per hour), burst rate, and IMI mean and standard deviation.

REM behavior disorder features analyze chin EMG during REM sleep. Tonic activity measures percentage of REM epochs with greater than 30 percent duration above 2 times NREM baseline. Phasic activity counts 0.1--5 second bursts above 4 times NREM baseline. Bruxism features detect 0.25--2 second bursts above 4 times background with bruxism episode index.

The final clinical feature matrix contains 136 features per subject after preprocessing steps including HRV zero-value replacement with median imputation, removal of near-zero variance features, and pruning of highly correlated duplicates ($|r| > 0.95$).
